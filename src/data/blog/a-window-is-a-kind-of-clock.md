A clock divides time into equal pieces. A window does something more interesting: it shows time changing the character of a place.

In the morning, light arrives low and direct. Dust becomes briefly architectural. By noon, the same room is flatter and more practical. Evening collects in corners before settling everywhere at once.

## Time has texture

Mechanical time is necessary, but it is not how a day feels. Ten minutes waiting and ten minutes absorbed in a task share a measurement without sharing much else.

Windows offer another scale. The patch of sun crossing a floor, a plant turning almost imperceptibly, reflections appearing after dark—these changes are imprecise but deeply legible. They remind us that time is not only spent. It is inhabited.

## Design for the day that moves

Rooms are often arranged as if light were fixed. A chair goes where it fits, a desk where there is an outlet. But noticing how a room changes can suggest a different arrangement.

Maybe the morning corner is better for reading. Maybe the table catches a useful winter light. Maybe one lamp, turned on before the room truly needs it, makes dusk feel less like a loss.

This is not optimization. It is acquaintance. The goal is to know a place well enough to cooperate with it.

The window keeps no appointments and rings no alarms. Still, it tells the time: not in numbers, but in qualities. Sometimes that is the time we need.
