We tend to imagine notes as a filing system: each idea labeled, placed, and retrieved in its original condition. My notes rarely behave so politely. They pile up. They contradict one another. Half of them only become interesting when they are next to something unrelated.

This can look like disorder. It can also be a kind of compost.

## Save fragments, not just conclusions

A polished insight is easy to keep because it already knows what it is. Fragments are more awkward: an overheard phrase, a question with no answer, a comparison that almost works.

Those are often the notes with the most potential. Because they are unfinished, they can still connect to something new.

The practical rule is simple: if a thought creates even a flicker of interest, write enough to recover the flicker. A sentence is usually plenty. The goal is not to prove the idea, only to keep it available.

## Return at a different speed

Reviewing notes immediately is useful for organizing. Reviewing them months later is useful for discovery. Distance changes the reader. What once seemed central may feel obvious; a discarded aside may suddenly belong to a project that did not exist when it was written.

I find three kinds of return especially helpful:

1. A quick weekly pass to add context before it disappears.
2. A slower monthly pass to connect related fragments.
3. An aimless occasional browse with no task to complete.

That last pass matters. Search retrieves what you know you need. Browsing gives the unknown a chance.

## Let the pile become soil

Not every note deserves a future. Many will simply break down, lending a word or a shape to something else. That is not failure. A notebook does not need to be a museum where every thought is preserved behind glass.

It can be a living system: slightly messy, regularly turned, and quietly making the next idea possible.
