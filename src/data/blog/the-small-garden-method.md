Large projects often begin with large abstractions: build the site, learn the skill, organize the room. The destination may be clear while the next honest action is not.

A small garden offers a better model. It has an edge. It contains different kinds of work. It changes gradually, but it changes where you can see it.

## Give the work a boundary

An unbounded project turns every possible improvement into a current obligation. A boundary makes care practical.

Instead of “organize the workshop,” choose one drawer. Instead of “learn to draw,” fill one page with the same object. Instead of “redesign everything,” make one screen genuinely pleasant to use.

Small is not the same as trivial. A garden bed may be small and still contain an entire season of attention.

## Tend before expanding

New ideas are exciting because they have no maintenance yet. Existing work is quieter. It asks to be revised, repaired, and sometimes removed.

The garden method gives tending equal status with starting. Before adding a feature, improve the one already there. Before buying another tool, sharpen the one on the bench. This keeps the project from growing faster than your ability to care for it.

## Leave evidence of progress

Visible progress creates its own energy. Keep a short changelog. Take a before photo. Put finished pieces somewhere you will encounter them.

The aim is not productivity theater. It is orientation. When effort accumulates slowly, evidence helps you remember that it is accumulating at all.

A bounded project can always become larger later. First, make one patch of ground healthy. Learn what grows there. Then decide where the path should go next.
